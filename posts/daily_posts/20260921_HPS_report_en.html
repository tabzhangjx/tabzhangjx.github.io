HPS: Turning Hard Negatives into a Preference-Alignment Sampling Distribution

Theory, evidence, and safety implications of Hard Preference Sampling

ICML 2025 / PMLR 267 2026-09-21 Preference Optimization · Alignment · Negative Sampling

HPS samples the rejected response most similar to the preferred one using an external ranker, widening implicit reward margins and saving training time while preserving external quality scores. But margin metrics closely mirror the objective; safety still needs independent red teaming and single-sample deployment evaluation.

Core positioning in one sentence. Hard Preference Sampling is a plug-in negative sampler, not a DPO replacement. External scalar rewards turn 99 rejected answers into an increasingly sharp distribution; one hardest-to-reject answer enters DPO/EXO/IPO/SPPO/NCA. Training falls from PL's 168.4 to 64.4 GPU-hours; HH-RLHF DPO implicit margin rises 0.349→2.723, while external Reward barely changes, 0.431→0.430.

Motivation: not missing negatives, but how negatives enter the loss

Preference training commonly compresses in two ways. BT reduces candidates to binary wins/losses against one rejected answer. PL preserves ranking but recursively prefers second over third and third over fourth. In safety contexts, if both are harmful, this temporarily places harmful content on the positive side. PL also forwards nn candidates and constructs nn sublosses, scaling cost linearly (§4.1, HTML lines 144–153).

Evidence has three layers. Compute: PL/BT/HPS fine-tuning times are 168.4±1.9、62.8±1.1、64.4±0.8 GPU-hour; HPS saves 104.0 GPU-hour / 61.76%over PL but costs 1.6 GPU-hour / 2.55%more than BT (§6, lines 314–316). Internal margin: HH-RLHF DPO-BT→DPO-HPS changes RM_DPO 0.349→2.723, +2.374/+680.23%; RM_R-DPO changes −0.455→2.040, crossing zero so percentage change is uninterpretable (Table 2, lines 240–242). External quality barely changes: Reward values are 0.431→0.430（−0.001，−0.23%）and BLEU 0.230→0.232（+0.002，+0.87%）. The strongest conclusion is greater internal separation at lower PL cost, not much higher external scores.

Data generation matters: each original prompt has only two human-ranked answers; RLHFlow/Llama3-v2-DPO expands this to 100 , ranked by Skywork-Reward-Llama3 (setup, lines 305–313). Rich candidates partly convert human preferences into reward-model-labeled synthetic preferences. Ranker errors, such as undervaluing safe refusals, are amplified more by HPS than random/uniform negatives.

Narrative, assumptions, and tradeoffs

Four steps summarize the approach:

  1. Redefine informational value. Rejected answers closest to preferred ones are hardest to distinguish and potentially most informative.
  2. Turn rankings into distributions. Fixed estimated rewards restr_{est} pass through temperature gammagamma softmax to form qq；gammagamma ; larger values approach hardest-negative sampling.
  3. Sample one negative. Rather than backpropagating through every candidate, draw from qq and pair it with the top response for ordinary pairwise optimization.
  4. Explain the limit. When gamma→∞gamma\to\inftySampling approaches worst-case negatives, and the limiting objective maximizes the minimum margin against the strongest rejected answer (Theorems 2–3, lines 207–227).

Assumptions span three areas: bounded implicit rewards, gradients, Hessians, and optimal parameters within radius BB (Assumption 1, lines 187–192); ranking by restr_{est} must align with true hardness; safety assumes every candidate in j≥2j\ge2 should be downweighted. Real preferences include equally safe styles, weak/nontransitive orders, and conflicting objectives; rejected is not synonymous with harmful.

The tradeoff is clear: HPS exchangesoffline candidate generation and external labeling costfor lowertraining-time backpropagation cost. Only one negative is backpropagated, but generating 100 answers and labeling on 8×L40S remains expensive; training uses 4×L40S (Appendix D.1, lines 732–736). The 61.76% reduction concerns fine-tuning duration, not total data-production-plus-training cost.

Equation-level formalization and workflow

For prompt xx and ranked candidates yτ(1)≻⋯≻yτ(n)y_{\tau(1)}\succ\cdots\succ y_{\tau(n)}, DPO-style implicit reward is

rθ(x,y)=βlogπθ(y∣x)πref(y∣x).r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y\mid x)}.

HPS defines a hardness distribution over rejected candidates (Eq.9, lines 173–178):

q(x,y)=exp(γrest(x,y))∑i=2nexp(γrest(x,yτ(i))),y∈{yτ(2),…,yτ(n)}.q(x,y)=\frac{\exp(\gamma r_{est}(x,y))}{\sum_{i=2}^{n}\exp(\gamma r_{est}(x,y_{\tau(i)}))},\qquad y\in\{y_{\tau(2)},\ldots,y_{\tau(n)}\}.

Substitute it into the preferred-versus-weighted-negatives objective (Eq.10):

ℒHPS=𝔼d∼𝒟−logerθ(x,yτ(1))erθ(x,yτ(1))+N𝔼y∼p(y)[erθ(x,y)q(x,y)],N=n−1.\mathcal L_{HPS}=\mathbb E_{d\sim\mathcal D}-\log\frac{e^{r_\theta(x,y_{\tau(1)})}}{e^{r_\theta(x,y_{\tau(1)})}+N\,\mathbb E_{y\sim p(y)}[e^{r_\theta(x,y)}q(x,y)]},\quad N=n-1.

The implementation does not calculate the inner expectation exactly, instead using y−∼qy^-\sim q single-sample Monte Carlo, retaining a DPO-like paired update. Theorem 3 gives the limiting objective

θ*=argmaxθ[rθ(x,yτ(1))−max1<j≤|τ|rθ(x,yτ(j))],\theta^*=\arg\max_\theta\left[r_\theta(x,y_{\tau(1)})-\max_{1<j\le|\tau|}r_\theta(x,y_{\tau(j)})\right],

as maximin margin (Eq.14, lines 224–227). Larger internal margins do not automatically lower real harmfulness; calibrated independent safety measures without shared model biases are missing.

for each prompt x:
    generate / retrieve n candidate responses
    keep y+ = rank-1 response
    score every rejected y_i with frozen r_est
    gamma <- linear schedule(-5, -3, -1, 1, 3, 5) at 20% intervals
    q_i <- softmax(gamma * r_est_i)
    sample one y- ~ q
    update chosen preference optimizer on (x, y+, y-)

Note the method statesgamma>1gamma>1, while experiments increase from −5 to 5 linearly(§4.2 line 176; implementation line 315). Negative gammagamma preferentially sampleslow-reward, easy-to-rejectanswers, opposite hard-negative sampling. This can be read as an easy-to-hard curriculum, but the transition is not integrated into the definition/theory.

Datasets, benchmarks, and metrics

HH-RLHF contains multi-turn helpful/harmless dialogues; the public expanded dataset card used by the authors lists train 161k rows, with a response sequence length of 3 per entry (dataset card lines 72–80).PKU-SafeRLHF / PKU-Safety is a single-turn safety preference dataset. Each prompt originally has two responses in this paper, subsequently expanded to 100. Thus, “sample count” includes both the number of prompts and candidates per prompt; 100 times as many candidates must not be treated as 100 times as many independent human annotations.

Four metric types: BLEU measures lexical overlap with reference answers and is only a weak proxy for open-ended dialogue; Reward comes from external ArmoRM-Llama3; RM_DPO is the difference in implicit log-ratios between preferred and rejected responses; RM_R-DPO additionally subtracts 0.01(|y+|−|y−|)0.01(|y^+|-|y^-|) to correct for length (Table 1, HTML lines 228–232). Human evaluation samples 15 prompts from each of the two test sets, totaling 30 questions and 4 system responses, rated by20 raters on a 1–5 Likert scale, anonymously and in randomized order (Section 6.1, lines 321–323). Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct provides 0–5 scores and pairwise win rates.

Layered ablations, hyperparameter sensitivity, and phase transitions

Candidate count. In the HH ablation of Table 6, DPO-HPS RM_DPO changes from n=5n=5 interval's 0.600 without JVP to n=100n=100 interval's 2.723, +2.123 or relative +353.83%; DPO-BT rises from 0.166 to 0.349, +0.183 or +110.24%. BLEU and Reward remain essentially flat. Expanding the support set mainly changes the rejection boundary, rather than the preferred answer’s wording or external RM quality. This also constitutes a phase transition: larger candidate pools are more likely to contain “high-scoring but incorrect/harmful” hard negatives, and HPS benefits grow with extreme-value statistics.

Transfer ablation. Appendix Table 7 gives PKU HPS RM_DPO values of 1.191, 1.620, 2.164, and 5.725 (n=5,20,50,100n=5,20,50,100). However, the n=50n=50 HPS row lists BLEU/Reward as 0.230/0.307, sharply inconsistent with neighboring rows around 0.31/0.407 (HTML lines 704–713), suggesting a typesetting or transcription error. No trend is inferred from these two cells until the authors clarify them.

Beta sensitivity. Appendix Table 8 gives β=0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\beta=0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,1. HPS RM_R-DPO values are 2.040, −0.828, −0.911, −0.925, −1.178; the value is positive only at 0.1. HPS exceeds BT at every beta, but “higher” does not mean “absolutely safe”: moving from 0.1 to 0.25 changes HPS RM_DPO from 2.723 to −0.146, a clear sign reversal. KL also falls from 11.767 to 6.972, indicating that the largest margin accompanies the greatest policy drift. The paper summarizes this as “all KL values outperform BT” (lines 730–731), without discussing this threshold.

gamma。 The paper provides only the −5→5 schedule and reports no ablations of fixed γ\gamma, schedule direction, or update frequency, nor tests of numerical stability for gamma→∞gamma\to\infty . Thus, experiments do not establish that “sharper hardness is always better”; the gap between the theoretical limit and the best engineering setting remains unexplored.

Gaps in component attribution. Key controls are missing: “100 candidates + uniform single sampling,” “deterministic top-1 from the same pool,” “easy-to-hard schedule alone,” and “oracle human hardness.” The observed gains therefore cannot be fully decomposed into five components: multiple candidates, external RM ranking, single sampling, temperature curriculum, and the HPS loss.

Statistical significance

The paper reports a mean±variation only for training time: PL 168.4±1.9、BT 62.8±1.1、HPS 64.4±0.8 GPU-hour. Main-table BLEU, Reward, margins, and human evaluation are point estimates; random-seed counts, standard deviations, confidence intervals, bootstrap estimates, paired tests, and multiple-comparison corrections are not reported. Although human evaluation includes 20 raters and 30 prompts, inter-rater agreement, question-level variance, and effect intervals are also absent. One can therefore say HPS scores higher in this run, but cannot rigorously claim that +0.11 Likert is statistically significant.

Three decimal places in Qwen win rates do not imply high precision. Without total sample counts, paired repetition structure, and judge position-bias estimates, the difference between 63.875% and 58.712% cannot be interpreted at the displayed precision. Best practice would apply paired bootstrap over prompts and separately report judge seeds, swapped response positions, and single-sample versus best-of-5 results.

Actual contributions, reusable designs, and transfer potential

The actual contribution is not another preference loss, but decomposing “listwise information” intoan offline ranking distribution + online single-negative updates. This lets any trainer accepting only (prompt, chosen, rejected) exploit 100 candidates without paying PL’s full backpropagation cost. A second contribution formalizes the hardest-negative max-margin intuition in alignment theory; a third demonstrates orthogonal plug-in use across DPO/EXO/IPO/SPPO/NCA.

Reusable designs include replacing qq with a safety classifier, factuality verifier, or multi-objective Pareto distribution; caching sampling probabilities with a small RM; setting gammagammaby domain or user group; and adding newly failed responses to the candidate pool online. In code generation,restr_{est} can use unit tests; mathematical reasoning can use a process verifier; medical QA can use a multi-objective score combining guideline consistency and refusal quality. In any high-risk domain, however, the definition of a hard negative must be calibrated with external empirical evidence; a “high model score” cannot directly mean “most dangerous.”

Limitations stated by the paper

  1. Evaluator budget (Section 7, HTML lines 337–340). The authors explicitly say budget constraints led them to estimate win rates with an open-source LLM; stronger GPT-4/Claude models might be more reliable. This limits the judge’s external validity.
  2. Compute constraints (Implementation, HTML lines 313–316). Compute limits restrict experiments to LoRA rank 8; conclusions for full-parameter training, larger models, and other architectures are therefore unverified.
  3. Not explicitly stated in Limitations, but directly bounded by the setup. Only HH-RLHF and PKU-SafeRLHF, one Llama3 reference family, and 2048-token contexts are used, leaving long contexts, multilingual use, and production red-teaming uncovered. The paper’s limitations section lists only one item; the additional two above are attributed to the main-text setup rather than presented as the authors’ own limitation statements.

Independent critique

  1. [Construct validity] Because RM_DPO is derived directly from logπθ/πreflog\pi_\theta/\pi_{ref} and HPS Theorem 3 and its training objective directly maximize that margin, part of the large margin increase reflects an objective–metric equivalence; it demonstrates successful optimization but cannot alone establish reduced real-world harmful generation.
  2. [External validity] Because all 100 candidates per prompt are expanded by one DPO model and ranked by Skywork RM, the actual human information still comes mainly from the original binary pairs. “Hard human preference” is thus more accurately “hard reward-model preference,” which may fail to transfer across generators or cultural groups.
  3. [Evaluation bias] Because each method samples 5 responses and reports the highest value for each metric (HTML line 313), whereas production usually returns one sample, Table 2 is a best-of-5 upper bound and cannot directly represent a single-sample deployment safety rate.
  4. [Hyperparameter robustness] Because HPS RM_R-DPO is 2.040 at β=0.1\beta=0.1 but becomes −0.828 at 0.25, and the largest margin corresponds to KL=11.767, the main conclusion depends on a narrow hyperparameter region. “Robust rejection” therefore needs comparison at fixed KL or fixed external quality.
  5. [Theory–implementation consistency] Because the method definition specifies gamma>1gamma>1but implementation starts at −5, negative temperature reverses the ranking and initially samples easy negatives. The hardest-distribution limit of Theorem 2 therefore does not cover the full training path, so the theory cannot explain gains during the first half of the curriculum.
  6. [Attribution] Because uniform single sampling, deterministic top-1, fixed gamma, and oracle ranking controls are absent, single-sample efficiency is not separated from hard weighting. The 64.4 GPU-hour cost and margin gains therefore cannot all be attributed to “hard sampling.”
  7. [Statistics] Because the main table and human evaluation lack variance/confidence intervals, human evaluation includes only 30 prompts, and DPO-HPS exceeds BT by just 0.11 Likert, the direction can be reported but the effect cannot be declared stable or significant.
  8. [Data consistency] Because Appendix Table 7’s n=50n=50 HPS BLEU/Reward of 0.230/0.307 is inconsistent with neighboring values of 0.31/0.407, at least two cells may contain transcription errors. Any “smooth scaling law” based on this point is therefore unreliable.

Related work: origins of the problem and technical lineage

DPO: reducing RLHF to binary classification

DPO addresses the complexity of “learn a reward, then run PPO” by directly optimizing chosen/rejected pairs through a closed-form reward parameterization; its arXiv abstract explicitly describes it as stable, lightweight, and requiring no LM sampling during training (2305.18290v3, lines 16–19). The remaining gap is that each update sees only one rejected response and assumes BT’s scalar, transitive preferences. HPS retains DPO’s implicit reward and trainer but compresses multiple rejected responses into an informative sampling distribution; DPO’s pairwise interface thus becomes HPS’s engineering entry point.

PRO: extending binary preferences to full rankings

PRO addresses pairwise feedback’s loss of global order through recursive comparisons of arbitrary-length rankings; its AAAI 2024 arXiv abstract describes aligning probability rankings with human rankings (2306.17492v2, lines 16–19). The remaining gap is precisely HPS’s starting point: PL/PRO requires a suboptimal harmful response to beat an even worse harmful response, and backpropagates through every candidate. HPS retains the information supplied by multiple candidates but rejects recursive ranking, constructing one-vs-hard-rest comparisons only around the preferred response.

LiPO: reweighting lists through learning-to-rank

LiPO systematizes preference optimization as listwise learning-to-rank and uses λ\lambda weights to distinguish preference pairs; its abstract reports outperforming DPO variants on multiple tasks (2402.01878v3, lines 16–20). The remaining gap is that full-list cost and the choice of negatives most worth updating remain embedded in the loss. HPS moves weighting into sampling, at the cost of retaining only one negative per step and potentially increasing gradient variance.

KTO: alignment from binary desirability labels

KTO explains preference optimization through prospect theory’s loss aversion and needs only desirable/undesirable labels; its ICML 2024 arXiv abstract emphasizes matching pairwise methods at 1B–30B, while explicitly noting that no HALO is universally best (2402.01306v5, lines 16–19). Its remaining gap is not exploiting relative hardness among rejected responses for the same prompt. HPS takes the opposite route: it leaves the optimizer’s behavioral-economic assumptions unchanged and modifies only the negative-data distribution. Combining them into HPS-KTO is a testable proposal.

Progress is not a monotonic “more ranking is better,” but movement within three constraints: preference information, backpropagation cost, and bias amplification. DPO reduces cost; PRO/LiPO recover information; HPS balances the two through importance sampling. The real shift moves listwise structure from inside the loss to the data-sampling layer.

Open challenges: actionable gaps for head-to-head experiments

Theory

  1. Consistency under noisy hardness. Fix generated candidates and the optimizer; compare oracle human rankings, Skywork RM, and RMs with 5/10/20% pair flips. Primary metrics should be actual human-rated unsafe rate, margin calibration error, and effective sample count in the theoretical bound. Test whether HPS becomes worse than uniform sampling beyond a noise threshold.
  2. Finite gamma rather than its limit. Compare fixed gamma∈{−5,−1,0,1,3,5,10}gamma\in\{-5,-1,0,1,3,5,10\}, the −5→5 schedule, and the reversed 5→−5 schedule; plot risk–quality Pareto frontiers at fixed KL to test whether gamma→∞gamma\to\infty is truly optimal rather than merely overfitting the RM.

Engineering and applications

  1. Fair end-to-end cost comparison. Under the same 8×L40S budget, compare PL-20, HPS-100, uniform-100, and online hard-negative mining. Total cost must include generation, RM annotation, caching, and training, with wall-clock time, GPU-hours, energy, and peak memory reported.
  2. Single-sample deployment safety. Sample only 1 response per prompt and report alongside the paper’s best-of-5. Use an independent safety classifier plus human red-teaming; report jailbreak success, over-refusal, helpfulness, and P95 latency. Only then can one assess whether margins translate into actual refusal capability.
  3. Scale transfer. Run head-to-head comparisons with the same candidate pool across 7B/13B/70B, LoRA/full-parameter training, and 2048/32k contexts. If HPS’s advantage disappears with model scale, it may be a small-model data-efficiency technique rather than a general alignment principle.

Emerging directions

  1. Multi-objective hard negatives. Use safety, factuality, and style RMs to construct three methods: scalar weighting, Pareto-front sampling, and random-objective sampling. Compare worst-group utility across population preferences to test whether a single restr_{est} flattens incommensurable preferences.
  2. Cyclic/intransitive preferences. Compare HPS, GPM/GPO, and BT-DPO on synthetic rock-paper-scissors preferences and real multi-user data. Measure cycle recovery, group Condorcet regret, and individual win rates to directly test HPS’s scalar-ranking assumption.

Recent-work overview

  1. Policy-labeled Preference Learning(ICML 2025) incorporates behavior-policy labels into a regret-based likelihood, addressing behavior-policy mismatch in offline preference data. It reminds us that HPS’s 100 candidates come from a specific generation policy and that generator information should not be discarded.
  2. AMPO(ICML 2025) uses active subset selection to cover reward extremes and semantic clusters. A direct comparison with HPS should fix subset size and distinguish “sampling only the hardest” from “also preserving diversity.”
  3. Energy-Based Preference Model / EPA(ICML 2025) notes that BT MLE may be non-unique and approximates energy models using strong negatives plus many free weak negatives. It challenges the scalar ranking underlying HPS from the perspective of preference-model identifiability.
  4. Beyond Bradley-Terry Models / GPM(ICML 2025) represents cyclic preferences with vector embeddings while retaining O(K) query complexity. It is suitable for testing whether HPS incorrectly forces a linear ordering on intransitive preferences.
  5. Right Now, Wrong Then(ICML 2025 PMLR volume contents) studies nonstationary DPO under preference drift. For HPS, cached qq can become invalid across populations or over time; timestamps and online re-estimation should be added.
  6. PoisonBench(ICML 2025 PMLR volume contents) evaluates preference-data poisoning. HPS’s concentrated sampling of high-scoring hard negatives may amplify attacks; test whether a few poisoned responses dominate qq。

Conclusion

HPS’s most valuable design takeaway is:Use multi-candidate information for sampling without sending every candidate through backpropagation. Under the same 8B/LoRA setup, it reduces PL fine-tuning time by 61.76% and enlarges implicit margins across multiple optimizers; its human rating of 3.93 also exceeds BT’s 3.82, indicating that it is more than a purely numerical exercise.

The key caution is equally clear: DPO-HPS HH Reward is 0.430, almost identical to BT’s 0.431; the largest numerical jump occurs in a margin directly shaped by the objective. Together with best-of-5, a narrow beta phase transition,gammagamma definition–implementation inconsistency, and missing significance tests, HPS should be positioned asan efficient hard-negative alignment framework, rather than a solution with a completed real-world safety validation loop.
