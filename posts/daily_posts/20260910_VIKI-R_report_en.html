2. Motivation: cooperation structure, not just actions, is the bottleneck

Single-robot VLAs often predict actions directly from images and instructions. Multi-robot cooperation adds three coupled decisions: which robots to activate, which subtasks can run in parallel, and whether each robot can reach targets without collisions. Final success rates conflate wrong embodiment selection, infeasible symbolic plans, and mistaken interpretation of partners' trajectories.

VIKI-Bench addresses fragmented coverage. Core Table 1 (p.3) lists only 6 RoCo tasks, 11 RoboFactory composition-constrained tasks, and LLaMAR/MAP-THOR with 225 tasks; although PARTNR contains 100,000 natural-language tasks, it primarily targets human–robot household collaboration. VIKI-Bench chooses an intermediate scale:100 scenes, 6 heterogeneous robot types, over 1,000 asset combinations, and 23,737 task instances, with both global and first-person views. Its distinction is not having the most tasks, but decomposing capabilities and providing programmatically verifiable supervision.

Zero-shot results show that this hierarchy is substantive. In core Table 2 (p.8), GPT-4o achieves only 18.40%L1 activation accuracy and an L2 average of 17.50%; although Gemini-2.5-Flash-preview reaches 31.40%on L1, its L2 OOD accuracy is only 10.51%. Qwen2.5-VL-7B achieves zero-shot L2 OOD accuracy of 0%. General VLMs may describe a scene yet almost never spontaneously produce multi-agent plans satisfying strict action syntax, preconditions, and step budgets.

Stronger external evidence comes from PARTNR: its 100,000 tasks span 60 houses and 5,819 objects; ReAct without privileged information achieves only 0.30success, versus 0.93for humans (PARTNR Table 2 and text, pp.10–11). Thus VIKI-R asks more than whether a VLM can write additional CoT: canverifiable collaboration constraintsbe converted into sufficiently dense post-training signals to move models from format compliance to compositional collaboration?

3. Argument, assumptions, and trade-offs

The paper follows three steps: decomposition, synthesis, and optimization.

  1. Decompose capabilities. L1 selects the required robot set; L2 produces parallel, parameterized action plans; L3 predicts 2D trajectories for itself and partners from one robot's first-person view. These correspond to team formation, symbolic coordination, and spatial anticipation.
  2. Synthesize data. Construct scenes using RoboCasa and ManiSkill3 assets, generate/refine plans with GPT-4o, then iteratively filter them through an Action Checker, World Simulator, and Plan Refiner. Supervision is not wholly manual; executable rules close the loop where possible.
  3. Two-stage learning. First apply SFT to CoT demonstrations so outputs enter the reward-recognizable region; then use GRPO to sample a group of answers for each input, reward format and task compliance, and reinforce relatively better samples.

The central assumption is that hierarchical rule rewards proxy real collaboration quality. L1 exact match assumes a unique correct team; L2 additionally assumes the ground-truth plan is near-optimal in length; L3 converts average trajectory distance into reward. Rewards are cheap, deterministic, and critic-free, but recognize only the benchmark's formal world. A realistic recovery plan one step longer than GT gets zero in L2; a 2D trajectory near the annotation can still score highly in L3 despite collisions or infeasible dynamics.

Cold start is another key tradeoff. DeepSeek-R1 showed reasoning can emerge from pure RL, but VIKI-R L2 barely learns from scratch: 3B/7B VIKI-R-Zero achieve L2 averages of only 0.00/0.10, rising with CoT cold start to 68.78/69.25(core Table 2, p.8). The more accurate account is therefore thatSFT brings the policy onto a verifiable planning manifold, and RL redistributes its probability mass, rather than RL spontaneously learning multi-robot collaboration.

4. Equation-level formalization, rewards, and algorithm

4.1 SFT cold start

Let the input state contain visual observations and the question s = (x,q), and let the CoT/answer sequence be y1 : T. The SFT objective is

$$ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)=-\mathbb E_{(x,q,y)}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log \pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,q,y_{<t}). $$

This is core Eq. (1), p.6. Each level is trained separately: L1 activation, L2 planning, and L3 trajectories do not share a unified policy head. Thus “hierarchical” describes the benchmark and training-task organization, not an end-to-end policy completing all three levels in one rollout.

4.2 Group-relative advantages and clipped objective

For the same state, sample G outputs from the old policy, {ai}i = 1G, and compute within-group normalized advantages:

$$ A_i=\frac{R_i-\bar R}{\sigma_R},\qquad r_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_i\mid s)}{\pi_0(a_i\mid s)}. $$

Then optimize

ℒclip = 𝔼i[min(riAi, clip(ri,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)Ai)],

J(θ) = ℒclip − βDKL(πθ∥π0).

These are Eqs. (2)–(5), pp.6–7. The notation is mildly ambiguous:π0 is described as serving both old/reference-policy roles, while the implementation hyperparameters use a KL-loss coefficient of 0.01. Reproduction must check whether rollout policy, pre-update old policy, and fixed reference model are actually the same object.

4.3 Hierarchical rewards

The total reward is

R = λ1Rformat + λ2Racc.

  • L1： Only when parsing succeeds and the predicted robot set exactly matches ground truth doesRacc = 1。
  • L2： A plan A must be judged feasible by the executor and have an action count N(A) ≤ Ngtto receive 1: RL2 = 1[feasible(A)]1[N(A)≤Ngt].
  • L3： For each agent, normalize RMSE, Hausdorff, and Dynamic Fréchet distances to d̂, transform them into 1 − d̂, then average across agents and metrics (Eq. (9), p.8).

These rewards have very different distributions: L1/L2 are approximately sparse Bernoulli signals, while L3 provides continuous shaped rewards. Pure RL can therefore improve from nonzero distance signals on L3, but on L2 it encounters almost no feasible samples to learn from.

4.4 Pseudocode

Input: level-specific data Dk, Qwen2.5-VL-Instruct, format checker, task verifier
1  SFT the backbone on each level's CoT cold-start subset to obtain πsft
2  For each training state s=(image, question):
3      Sample G=5 answers a1...aG from the old policy for the same s
4      Parse answers; malformed outputs receive only format penalty/zero task reward
5      L1: exact-match robot set
6      L2: Action Checker → World Simulator; require feasibility and no more steps than GT
7      L3: compute multi-agent RMSE/HD/DFD and normalize into continuous reward
8      Normalize rewards within the group to obtain Ai
9      Update policy with clipped ratios + KL regularization
10 Repeat for the prescribed epochs; export separate L1/L2/L3 checkpoints
Output: one VIKI-R model per level, not a closed-loop three-level controller

The three distances have different semantics. RMSE penalizes pointwise deviations; Hausdorff measures the worst nearest-point distance; Dynamic Fréchet also preserves curve order. The paper averages them into AVG. Although pixel-based/normalized, their scales do not establish that equal weights reflect control risk. More fundamentally, L3 is trajectory perception/prediction, not a policy rollout. It may support collision avoidance, but does not establish multi-robot trajectory coordination.

8. Layered ablations, hyperparameter sensitivity, and transitions

8.1 Step penalty: large on a challenging subset, small on the full split

Main-text Table 4 (p.9) reports OOD-H/ID-H on “challenging L2” without the step penalty as 7.1/8.0, and with it as 46.8/96.0, an absolute gain of 39.7/88.0 pp, while average excess steps fall from 1.97 to 0.05. Yet on the full L2 split in Appendix Table 7 (p.26), the same ablation gives only ID 92.06→95.22（+3.16 pp）、OOD 29.51→33.25（+3.74 pp）. This is not contradictory, but indicates gains concentrated in specially selected difficult cases. The challenging subset's selection criteria and size are unspecified, so 88 pp cannot represent the overall effect.

8.2 Model scale: different transitions at different levels

Appendix Table 8 (p.26) gives 3B/7B/32B L1 scores of 74.10/93.00/95.30and L2 scores of 68.78/69.25/74.74. Scaling 3B→7B adds 18.90 pp on L1 but only 0.47 pp on L2; 7B→32B mainly improves L2 (+5.49 pp), with only +2.30 pp on L1. This suggests, without proving, two transitions: team selection approaches saturation near 7B, while compositional planning needs more capacity. Denser scale points or compute-matched curves are absent.

8.3 Prompt guidance and optimizer

Appendix Table 9 (p.26): removing guidance prompts reduces 3B L1 from 74.10 to 73.47 (−0.63 pp) and 7B from 93.00 to 92.95 (−0.05 pp). The authors report slower convergence but no steps-to-threshold. Prompts thus appear to accelerate optimization rather than determine final performance.

Appendix Table 10 (p.26) compares GRPO and DAPO: 3B ID/OOD is 93.61/32.11 for GRPO and 93.22/33.33for DAPO; 7B is 95.22/33.25 for GRPO and 95.44/33.58for DAPO. DAPO wins three of four metrics by 0.22–1.22 pp. Without variance, this does not establish significance, but it shows GRPO itself is not the main novelty; verifiable hierarchical rewards and cold-start data are central.

8.4 Training curves reveal a reward-support transition

Appendix Figures 5–7 (pp.24–25) support only visual estimates, so all following values are approximate. On L2, 3B R-Zero reward rises from about 0.009 to 0.019 before declining, while 7B stays around 0.06–0.075; with SFT both reach about 0.9 by roughly 60 steps. L3 differs: 7B R-Zero ends around 0.80 versus about 0.75 for full VIKI-R, so pure RL is even higher. L2 has a discrete feasibility gate, while L3 uses continuous distances. Whether cold start is needed depends on the initial policy's coverage of nonzero rewards—a more transferable insight than “CoT always helps.”

8.5 Statistical significance

not reported by the paperseed counts, mean±standard deviation, bootstrap CIs, or significance tests. Table 2 also lacks episode-level predictions, so this report cannot add paired tests. Large differences, such as 7B L2 R-Zero 0.10→VIKI-R 69.25, have a credible direction; differences of 0.05, 0.22, 0.47, or 1.12 pp cannot establish stable superiority.

9. Related-work chain: problem solved → remaining gap → VIKI-R response → inherited design

9.1 RoCo: from language negotiation to executable multi-arm trajectories

RoCo uses LLM dialogue to form high-level multi-robot strategies and passes waypoints to a low-level motion planner. RoCoBench has only six task types, but includes collision feedback and real multi-arm demonstrations. Table 2 (p.5) reports success/steps/replans over 20 runs per task; real-world settings use ten runs each (Table 3, p.7). Remaining gaps are small scale, expensive in-context LLM dialectics, and no unified activation–planning–trajectory diagnosis. VIKI-R replaces dialogue loops with an offline-learned policy, translating environment-based plan correction into Action Checker/World Simulator rewards, but drops the real execution loop.

9.2 LLaMAR: a long-horizon plan–act–correct–verify loop

LLaMAR addresses partially observed, long-horizon multi-agent planning using Planner, Actor, Corrector, and Verifier modules in MAP-THOR/SAR. Its abstract/conclusion reports approximately 30% success-rate improvement over prior planners (pp.1,11; full Table 2 on p.9). MAP-THOR contains only 225 tasks and relies primarily on inference-time module orchestration. VIKI-R inherits verifier-generated structural feedback but moves it from inference-time correction into training-time rewards, reducing interaction costs per inference. The tradeoff is lacking LLaMAR-style online replanning when the environment changes.

9.3 PARTNR: scaled human–robot collaboration exposes tracking/recovery failures

PARTNR uses an LLM with a simulator in the loop to generate 100,000 natural-language tasks across 60 houses and 5,819 objects (p.1, Table 1 p.3). It targets large-scale household human–robot planning and finds ReAct success of 0.30 without privileged perception versus 0.93 for humans; an 8B fine-tuned planner approaches 70B performance while being 8.6×faster (Table 2 and text, pp.10–11). Gaps remain in hierarchical visual-collaboration diagnosis and diverse robot embodiments. VIKI-R transfers simulator-verified generation to a multi-embodiment visual setting, adding team activation and partner-trajectory perception, but reduces scale from 100k to 23.7k and offers weaker evidence for real human–robot interaction.

9.4 RoboFactory: compositional constraints make generated data safe and feasible

RoboFactory automates heterogeneous multi-agent manipulation data generation with explicit viewpoint, spatial, and temporal compositional constraints. Its 11 tasks compare data quantities and shared/separate architectures using diffusion policies and imitation learning (Tables 2/3, p.7). It is closer to low-level imitation/control, with limited coverage of why agents are selected or how long plans and OOD are evaluated. VIKI-R directly reuses the MARS lineage, lifting compositional constraints into verifiable symbolic rewards and expanding to three cognitive levels, but does not retain RoboFactory's actual action-success evaluation loop.

9.5 DeepSeek-R1: GRPO and cold start are not robot-specific inventions

DeepSeek-R1 addresses LLM reasoning post-training using group rewards instead of a value model and rule-based accuracy/format rewards; see GRPO in §2.1 (p.2) and PPO comparisons in Appendix A.3 (pp.16–18). Mathematics/code answers are naturally verifiable but lack vision, physics, and multi-agent feasibility. VIKI-R's transfer is direct: group-relative advantages, clipping, KL, and format/task rewards remain; the new elements are three embodied verifiers. Its contribution is transplanting verifiable RL into a hierarchical collaboration benchmark, not inventing a new RL algorithm.

10. Contributions, reusable designs, and transfer potential

There are three genuine contributions. First,evaluation decompositionseparates team selection, symbolic planning, and trajectory perception, locating structural failures such as selecting the right agents but failing to schedule parallel steps. Second,rule interfacesturn format checkers, Action Checkers, World Simulators, and trajectory distances directly into rewards without training another black-box reward model. Third,reward-support diagnosisshows, somewhat unexpectedly, that discrete feasibility rewards need cold start while continuous distance rewards can support pure RL—a methodological insight more valuable than one SOTA score.

The most reusable design is not the particular Qwen/GRPO recipe, but “write the verifier first, then choose the post-training method”:

  • For exact-match team formation, use set rewards and separately report precision/recall to detect overactivation.
  • For compositional plans, verify feasibility with an executor and treat length, collision risk, and energy as multiple reward objectives, rather than using only N(A) ≤ Ngt as a hard gate.
  • For sparse rewards, use a small number of CoT/trajectory demonstrations to enter the support; for continuous rewards, compare pure RL to reduce teacher-imitation bias.
  • Retain LLaMAR/RoCo-style feedback at deployment: let the learned policy propose and the verifier correct, rather than compressing the entire loop into one generation.

Real-robot transfer needs three further layers: camera calibration and occlusion uncertainty, dynamics/safety controllers, and communication latency/agent failure. For the user's current Agent Memory interest, the analogy is to make agent/fact selection, parallel planning, and collaborator-state prediction separately verifiable, then use continuous/discrete reward-support properties to decide on cold start. VIKI-R itself has no long-term memory, conflicting-fact updates, or cross-session evaluation.

11. Stated limitations and independent critique

11.1 Authors' limitations

  1. Simulation limitations: the first Limitations paragraph (pp.22–23) acknowledges primarily simulated experiments and a fixed hierarchy unable to cover unexpected obstacles, changing goals, or adaptive agent behavior. Open-world extrapolation therefore requires caution.
  2. Compute cost: the second paragraph acknowledges expensive multi-level fine-tuning. Table 12 (p.28) gives 36 hours on 8×A800 for all three 7B levels, without comprehensive cost accounting.
  3. Limited interpretability: the third paragraph acknowledges insufficient study of reasoning interpretability in complex scenes. CoT currently serves as a training/output format, not a faithful causal trace.

11.2 Independent critique: explicit “because X, therefore Y” arguments

  1. [RL attribution/statistics] Because 7B VIKI-R falls below Ans-SFT on L2 ID by 1.67 ppand 3B L3 Avg worsens by 0.78, DAPO slightly beats GRPO on three of four L2 values (Table 10, p.26), and the main table lacks seeds, standard deviations, or confidence intervals, neither universal cross-level improvement nor small differences can be uniquely attributed to GRPO. The gains more plausibly reflect data, verifiers, and RL together.
  2. [Missing strong baselines] Because there are no compute-matched longer-SFT, SFT+best-of-5, rejection-sampling, or all-level DPO/DAPO comparisons, while GRPO samples five rollouts per prompt, the gains cannot be separated into policy-gradient effects versus extra sampling and rule filtering.
  3. [Real-world extrapolation] Because the real-photo experiment in Table 5 includes only four zero-shot general VLMs andno VIKI-R, “beyond simulation” does not establish sim-to-real transfer by the proposed method. At most, it shows L1 can be transferred to real photos and remains difficult.
  4. [Human comparability] Because VIKI-R trails humans on L2 by 27.25 ppand has L3 errors higher by 25.33%–34.36%(Table 6, p.10), “human-comparable” must be restricted to L1 activation, not overall capability.
  5. [Reward validity] Because L2 assigns zero to every executable plan satisfying N(A) > Ngt , while alternative embodiments, recovery, or safer plans may naturally take longer, the reward conflates matching GT efficiency with task correctness and may suppress diverse, reasonable collaboration strategies.
  6. [Data/statistical definitions] Because the paper gives L2 counts of both 10,714(Appendix C.1) and 10,174(Appendix D), and its 23,737 total differs from HF's 22,637 rows by 1,100, overlap between splits and cold-start data cannot be reproduced unambiguously. Numerical coincidence is a clue, not a substitute for a data card.
  7. [Ablation selection bias] Because the step penalty improves an unspecified challenging subset by 39.7/88.0 ppbut the full ID/OOD splits by only 3.16/3.74 pp, its headline effect depends on subset selection. The subset list and preregistered difficulty criteria are needed.
  8. [Static tasks substituted for a closed loop] Because L1/L2/L3 are trained and evaluated separately, L3 outputs only annotated 2D trajectories, and no joint select-agents→execute→observe→replan rollout is shown, the evidence establishes hierarchical visual-reasoning benchmark performance, not end-to-end multi-robot collaboration success.

12. Open challenges that can become head-to-head experiments

12.1 Theory

  1. A theorem for sparse reward support. Fix Qwen2.5-VL, data, and 288 A800-GPU-hours; compare RL-zero, SFT→GRPO, curriculum rewards, and continuous feasibility scores on L2. Plot initial nonzero-reward probability against final OOD accuracy to test for a cold-start transition.
  2. Is the GT-length gate consistent? On the same feasible plans, compare the hard gate 1[N≤Ngt], soft length penalties, and Pareto rewards (success/safety/energy). Have three robotics experts blindly rate diverse plans and measure reward–preference Kendall τ。
  3. Within-group normalization stability. Under G ∈ {2, 5, 8, 16} , report the proportion of σR = 0 , effective gradient batches, KL, and OOD performance. Compare directly with critic-based PPO to determine whether GRPO's memory savings sacrifice stability on sparse levels.

12.2 Engineering applications

  1. Unified closed-loop control. On the same 100 unseen scenes, compare three chained VIKI-R checkpoints, a shared backbone with jointly trained heads, and a LLaMAR feedback planner. Report task success, collisions, steps, p95 latency, and recovery counts.
  2. Physical-robot sim-to-real. Use at least three physical robot types, 20 tasks, and ten trials per task to compare base Qwen, Ans-SFT, VIKI-R, and VIKI-R+online verifier. Separate lighting, occlusion, and camera-pose perturbations and report paired bootstrap CIs instead of only successful videos.
  3. Compute-matched post-training. Fix the 7B budget at 288 A800-GPU-hours and compare SFT, SFT+best-of-5, rejection fine-tuning, DPO, DAPO, and GRPO with equal generated-token counts. Primary metrics: L2 OOD and real closed-loop success.
  4. Audit dataset counts. Build manifests for the paper's 23,737 examples, both appendix L2 counts, and HF's 22,637 rows. Check cold-start/train/test overlap by unique sample ID, publish hashes and deduplication scripts, then rerun Table 2.

12.3 Emerging directions

  1. Dynamic teams and agent failure. Randomly disable one robot at 30%/60% task progress and compare static VIKI-R, online replanning, and communication-aware MARL. Measure recovery success, unnecessary activations, and replanning tokens.
  2. Memory-enhanced collaboration. Provide cross-episode robot reliability/history and compare no memory, vector memory, and dynamic graph memory. Inject stale-fact conflicts and capability updates; measure team-selection accuracy, incorrect fact citations, and update latency.
  3. 3D/probabilistic trajectories. Compare 3D probabilistic trajectories with 2D point predictions under occlusion, reporting ADE/FDE, NLL, and collision-calibration ECE. Test whether low 2D error actually reduces physical collisions.

13. Seven recent works to track

  1. **Robot-R1（NeurIPS 2025）**: applies RL to embodied reasoning and control, closer to action execution than VIKI-R. Examine whether rewards truly close the loop and whether SFT is compute-matched.
  2. **ERA（ICLR 2026 OpenReview）**: directly studies RL paths from VLMs to embodied agents. Recheck OpenReview identity and final version before citation.
  3. **SimpleVLA-RL**: explores scalable VLA reinforcement fine-tuning, a nearby comparison for whether verifiable text planning transfers to continuous actions.
  4. **RDT-2**: reinforcement fine-tuning for generalist robot policies; compare safety constraints needed for VIKI-R's symbolic rewards versus action-level rewards.
  5. **π0.5**: focuses on open-world generalization, reminding us that VIKI-R OOD is a symbolic/scene split, not open-world deployment.
  6. **RoboTwin 2.0**: expands data generation and benchmarking through strong domain randomization, potentially supplementing VIKI-R sim-to-real experiments.
  7. **Tool-RoCo**: treats other agents as tools and evaluates self-organization, reporting cooperative tools at only 7.09% versus activation tools at 96.42% (abstract). It targets when to deactivate partners or seek help beyond VIKI-L1's exact-set metric.

14. Conclusion

VIKI-R merits attention not because GRPO supposedly brings robots to human level, but because it decomposes multi-agent visual collaboration into verifiable, trainable, auditable hierarchical interfaces. Its strongest result is 7B L2 OOD versus Ans-SFT rising from 25.62% to 33.25% (+7.63 pp, +29.78%), alongside L1 from 72.20% to 93.00%. Key counterevidence is the 1.67 pp L2 ID decline, 0.78 regression on 3B L3, and absence of VIKI-R in real-world experiments. The justified conclusion is:CoT cold start plus hierarchical rule rewards can substantially expand the support of feasible plans, but three levels of static reasoning have not been shown equivalent to real closed-loop collaboration.
